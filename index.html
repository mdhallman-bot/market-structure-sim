Introduction
What Kind of Market Are You In?
Before you run a business, you need to understand the world it operates in. Not all markets are the same — and the type of market you're in changes everything.

A Tale of Two Businesses

Imagine two scenarios. In the first, you're a wheat farmer. Your product is identical to every other wheat farmer's product. The price is set by the market — you take it or leave it. If you charge even a penny more, every buyer goes to someone else. You have no power.

In the second scenario, you're the only company that makes a life-saving drug that keeps people alive. You set the price. If someone needs it, they pay. No competition, no alternatives, no ceiling.

Both are legal businesses operating in the United States. But they exist in completely different market structures — and those structures determine how much power you have, how you compete, and how much profit you can earn.

The Spectrum of Market Power

Economists organize markets into four structures based on how competitive they are. At one end is perfect competition — thousands of sellers, no individual power. At the other end is monopoly — one seller, total control. In between are monopolistic competition and oligopoly.

In this simulation, you'll learn each structure, apply your knowledge, and then step into the shoes of a business owner and make real decisions in the structure you choose.

💡 Think About It

Would you rather be one of 10,000 identical wheat farmers selling at a price you can't control — or the only company making a drug millions of people need? As you go through this simulation, think about who benefits and who loses in each type of market.

Framework
The 4 Key Characteristics
Every market structure can be analyzed using four lenses. Learn these — they're the framework you'll use throughout the entire simulation.
01

Number of Sellers

Is there one seller, a few, or thousands? The more sellers, the more competitive the market and the less power any one firm holds.

Example: Thousands of corn farmers vs. one local electric utility

02

Product Type

Are all products in the market identical (homogeneous), or does each firm's product feel different (differentiated)? Differentiation creates brand loyalty and pricing power.

Example: Wheat (identical) vs. smartphones (differentiated)

03

Barriers to Entry

How hard is it for a new business to enter the market? Low barriers mean more competition over time. High barriers protect existing firms from challengers.

Example: Opening a lemonade stand (easy) vs. launching a new airline (extremely hard)

04

Price Control

Does the firm set its own price (price maker) or does the market set it for them (price taker)? This is often the most important characteristic for understanding profit potential.

Example: A commodity farmer (price taker) vs. a pharmaceutical company with a patent (price maker)

Why These Four?

These four characteristics work together to define a market structure. A market with many sellers, identical products, no barriers, and no price control is perfect competition. A market with one seller, a unique product, massive barriers, and full price control is a monopoly. The structures in between mix these characteristics in different ways — and those differences have enormous consequences for consumers, businesses, and society.

Market Structure 1 of 4
Perfect Competition
The most competitive market possible — and the one where individual firms have the least power.

🌾 Real-World Anchor: The Wheat Farmer

Imagine you grow wheat on a 200-acre farm in Kansas. So do 50,000 other farmers across the country. Your wheat is chemically identical to theirs. When harvest season comes, you all sell on the same commodity exchange, and the price is whatever the market sets that day. You cannot charge more — buyers will go elsewhere. You cannot charge less — you'd lose money for no reason. You are a price taker.

The Four Characteristics in Perfect Competition

Many sellers: Hundreds of thousands of farmers, producers, or firms all competing simultaneously.

Identical (homogeneous) products: One bushel of #2 hard red wheat is the same as any other. No branding, no loyalty, no "premium" version.

No barriers to entry: Anyone with land and seeds can start farming. New producers can enter the market freely, which prevents any single firm from gaining long-term power.

No price control: The market price is set by supply and demand across all buyers and sellers. Individual firms are too small to influence the price — they accept it as given.

Long-Run Equilibrium: Profit = Zero

Here's the surprising part: in the long run, perfectly competitive firms earn zero economic profit. If farmers are earning good profits, new farmers enter the market, increasing supply and pushing the price down until profits disappear. If farmers are losing money, some exit, decreasing supply and pushing the price back up. The market always self-corrects.

This doesn't mean farmers earn nothing — zero economic profit means they earn just enough to cover all costs including the cost of their own time and capital. It's a break-even situation.

💡 Did You Know?

The Chicago Mercantile Exchange sets commodity prices for wheat, corn, soybeans, and cattle in real time based on global supply and demand. On a single day in 2022, wheat prices jumped 30% due to Russia's invasion of Ukraine — and every wheat farmer in the world woke up either significantly richer or poorer based on decisions made thousands of miles away. That's perfect competition.

Market Structure 2 of 4
Monopolistic Competition
Many sellers, but each one offers something slightly different — and that difference is everything.

🌮 Real-World Anchor: The Taco Restaurant

Cruz's Tacos is one of 15 taco restaurants in a mid-sized city. They all sell tacos — but Cruz's has a secret green salsa recipe, a loyalty app, a cozy atmosphere, and a devoted following. The restaurant down the street has cheaper prices but smaller portions. A third uses only organic ingredients. They all sell "tacos," but none of them are selling the exact same thing.

The Four Characteristics in Monopolistic Competition

Many sellers: Lots of firms compete — think restaurants, clothing stores, salons, coffee shops. The market isn't dominated by any one player.

Differentiated products: Each firm's product is slightly different in some way — branding, quality, location, atmosphere, or features. This is called product differentiation.

Low barriers to entry: It's not hard to open a restaurant or clothing boutique. New competitors can enter the market if it looks profitable.

Some price control: Because Cruz's has loyal customers who prefer their salsa, they can charge a bit more than a generic competitor. But not too much — substitutes exist.

The Role of Non-Price Competition

In monopolistic competition, firms rarely compete purely on price. Instead, they use non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty programs, interior design, social media, and innovation. The goal is to make customers feel that your version of the product is worth more — or simply irreplaceable.

In the long run, like perfect competition, economic profits trend toward zero as new firms enter. But unlike wheat farming, each firm maintains a unique identity — which creates a small degree of lasting market power.

💡 Did You Know?

The average American lives within 3 miles of a McDonald's. McDonald's doesn't compete on price — a Big Mac costs roughly the same as similar burgers nearby. What they sell is familiarity: the same taste, the same packaging, the same experience in every city. That consistency is the differentiation. It's why they spend over $800 million a year on advertising.

Market Structure 3 of 4
Oligopoly
A small number of large firms dominate — and every move one makes sends ripples through all the others.

✈️ Real-World Anchor: The Airline Industry

In most U.S. cities, only 2–4 major airlines serve each route. When Delta drops fares on a Chicago–Atlanta flight, United and American match within hours — sometimes minutes. When fuel prices rise, they all add surcharges at roughly the same time. They watch each other constantly, react to each other constantly, and their decisions are deeply intertwined. This is interdependence — the defining feature of oligopoly.

The Four Characteristics in Oligopoly

Few large sellers: A handful of firms control the majority of the market. Examples: airlines, phone carriers, auto manufacturers, streaming services, breakfast cereal brands.

Products can be identical or differentiated: Steel and oil (sold by a few large producers) are nearly identical. Airlines, phones, and cars are differentiated.

High barriers to entry: You can't just start a new airline or car company. The capital costs, infrastructure requirements, regulatory hurdles, and existing brand loyalty make it extremely hard for new firms to enter.

Significant price control — but with interdependence: Each firm has real pricing power, but can't act without considering how rivals will respond.

Collusion, Cartels, and Price Wars

Because there are so few firms, they sometimes secretly cooperate — called collusion — to fix prices and divide markets. When a group of firms formally coordinate this way, it's called a cartel. OPEC, the oil-producing nations' alliance, is one of history's most powerful cartels.

Collusion is illegal in the United States under antitrust law. But the temptation is strong: why compete and drive prices down when you can all agree to keep them high? When collusion breaks down, the result is often a brutal price war where firms slash prices to steal market share from each other.

💡 Did You Know?

In 2016, the European Union fined Apple $14.5 billion for a sweetheart tax deal with Ireland that allowed Apple to pay an effective corporate tax rate of 0.005%. Only a firm with oligopoly-level market power — making hundreds of billions and employing tens of thousands across a continent — could negotiate a deal like that with a sovereign government. The EU ruled it was illegal state aid. Apple appealed, and the case dragged through courts for eight years.

Market Structure 4 of 4
Monopoly
One seller. No competition. Total control — and total responsibility.

💧 Real-World Anchor: The Water Utility

In your city, there is exactly one company that delivers water to your tap: ClearFlow Water Co. You cannot switch to a competitor — there isn't one. If ClearFlow raises rates, you pay. You cannot go without water. This is a natural monopoly: the infrastructure costs of building a second water system are so enormous that it makes no economic sense for a competitor to enter the market.

The Four Characteristics in Monopoly

One seller: A single firm serves the entire market. There are no direct competitors.

Unique product with no close substitutes: You can't easily replace tap water, electricity, or a patented life-saving drug with something else.

Very high barriers to entry: Legal barriers (patents, government licenses), technological barriers (infrastructure), and cost barriers (scale requirements) all prevent new entrants.

Full price control — the firm is a price maker: A monopolist sets whatever price maximizes its profit, constrained only by how much demand will fall at higher prices.

Types of Monopolies

Natural monopoly: The market naturally produces one dominant firm because of high infrastructure costs (water, electricity, railways).

Government monopoly: The government grants exclusive rights — the U.S. Postal Service has a legal monopoly on first-class mail delivery.

Technological monopoly: A company holds a patent that prevents competition (pharmaceutical companies, tech innovations).

Because monopolies harm consumers through high prices, reduced innovation, and limited choice, governments use antitrust law and regulation to control them.

💡 Did You Know?

Standard Oil, founded by John D. Rockefeller, once controlled 91% of U.S. oil refining. It used predatory pricing — temporarily selling below cost — to drive competitors out of business, then raised prices once they were gone. In 1911, the Supreme Court ruled it an illegal monopoly and broke it into 34 separate companies. Many of those companies still exist today: ExxonMobil, Chevron, and BP are all descendants of Standard Oil.

The Big Pattern

Notice how the characteristics move in one direction as you go from perfect competition to monopoly: fewer sellers → more differentiation → higher barriers → more price control → higher potential profits. Each step along the spectrum represents a shift in power — away from consumers and toward producers.

Big Picture
Who Benefits?
Market structures don't just describe how businesses operate — they determine who wins and who loses in the economy.

🌾 Perfect Competition → Best for Consumers

Prices are as low as possible. No firm has power to overcharge. Consumers get the full benefit of competition. The downside: firms earn minimal profits and have little incentive to invest in innovation.

🌮 Monopolistic Competition → Good for Consumers

Prices are reasonable, and consumers get variety and choice. Firms differentiate to attract customers, which drives quality and innovation. The slight inefficiency: resources spent on advertising could theoretically go elsewhere.

✈️ Oligopoly → Mixed Results

Large firms can invest in innovation (smartphones, aircraft). But the risk of collusion, price-fixing, and consumer harm is significant. Government regulation is critical in oligopolistic markets.

💧 Monopoly → Worst for Consumers

The monopolist charges higher prices, produces less output, and has little incentive to innovate. Consumers have no alternatives. Without government regulation, monopolies can cause significant harm.
